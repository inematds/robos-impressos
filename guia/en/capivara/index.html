In short

The capybara is a 30 cm robot for you to print at home and assemble. It rolls on two wheels hidden in its haunches and two support balls at the front, under its paws; its head turns 60° to each side and tilts; its mouth opens. Inside are a Raspberry Pi, an Arduino, three servos, two motors, and a battery that charges via USB-C. You need a 3D printer with a 256 mm build plate, the parts on the shopping list, and a soldering iron. This page covers phase 1: the assembled body, connected wires, and centred servos. Programming (walking, voice, camera, AI) is phase 2 and has not been done yet.

The robot

What will roll around your home

A 30 cm pear-shaped capybara, with a wide head resting against its body and a green bandana. From the front, no screw or technical hole is visible: only the nostrils and pupils.

Inside

A PETG chassis carries everything heavy: the 2 motors with wheels, the battery, the boards in 2 trays that slide out to the rear, and the servo that turns the head. The coloured shell fits over it. The mouth servo goes in the head, along with the camera and sensors in phase 2.

What supports the weight

The parts that take the strain are made of PETG: chassis, trays, neck fork, tilt cradle, and head plate. The shell is made of PLA and just covers the robot. Six parts need supports, always on the inside or underneath: none on the face.

↔️ Rolls on 2 wheels

Two geared motors, one in each wheel, are hidden in the haunches. Two steel support balls at the front, under the paws, complete the support.

↕️ Turns and tilts its head

The head turns 60° to each side and tilts from −15° to +20°, on a ball hidden inside the collar. Both servos are inside the neck.

🙂 Opens its mouth

A micro servo inside the head opens the mouth up to 18°. The jaw snaps into place without a screw on the face.

The project checker checks balance and clearance between parts with the head and mouth in 8 positions. The result is at the end of this page.

⚠️ Risks to consider before buying

What hasn't been tested yet. Read before spending money.

  • Nothing has been printed or assembled yet. The wiring has not been bench-tested: it was put together from the component datasheets.
  • Six parts need supports: skull, body, rear door, jaw, neck fork and tilt cradle. The supports go inside or underneath, away from the face, but no one has removed them from a real part yet.
  • The 3MF files for the build plates have never been opened in Bambu Studio. If one won’t open, use the STL files.
  • The on/off switch is rated for 3 A, and based on the datasheets, normal use comes close to that (about 2.5 A with the wheels moving and the head moving). The 3 A fuse protects the switch and may blow when the robot strains. A larger switch would require changing the part cutout: this is an open project task.
  • The Raspberry Pi gets 5.1 V through the 5 V pins because its USB-C port sits against the rack wall in the tray. This is a common connection, but it has not been tested in this robot: swapped wires or the wrong voltage on these pins can burn out the Pi. Measure before connecting it.
  • There is about 19 mm left above the Raspberry Pi up to the top tray (model measurement, unconfirmed). A standard Dupont connector is about 16.5 mm tall: it fits with little clearance. Plan B: solder the wires to a low female pin header, with the wires coming out sideways.
  • The rear door stays attached to the robot by the charger's 3 wires, which pass through a 7 mm hole in it. To remove the door completely, you need to desolder these wires or add a 3-pin connector, which is not on the list.
  • The test coupon does not check the MG996R servo window. Check the servo in the tilt cradle, which comes off one of the first build plates, before starting the long prints.
  • The fuse holder, terminal block, and 3 serial resistors have no modeled place: they sit loose inside the body, insulated with heat-shrink tubing.
  • Cable lengths (head servos, extensions, and jumpers) were estimated from the model. The head rotates and tilts, pulling these cables: leave slack and check both ends before closing it up.
  • The robot rests on 4 points (2 wheels and 2 balls). On an uneven floor, one ball may be off the ground and the robot may wobble.
  • Measurements marked NC come from store listings. Measure your part before printing the section that touches it.
  • The micro servo spline has steps of about 17° (typical value, not confirmed): the mouth horn may not land exactly at 90°. Choose the closest tooth; fine-tune it in the program in phase 2.
  • Heat: the Raspberry Pi is enclosed in the body, which is made of PLA and has no ventilation openings. PLA softens near 60 °C, and the Pi starts throttling at 80 °C. Glue on the heatsinks and check with the vcgencmd measure_temp command. If it goes above 70 °C, make ventilation holes in the rear door.

The process

From file to robot in 4 steps

The order matters: the first print takes less than an hour and helps you avoid losing a whole day of printer time.

1
Print the test pieceA small coupon with threaded insert holes in three sizes, screw holes, the micro servo slot, and the motor shaft hole. You can find out what fits your printer before using up filament.
2
Print the 11 build platesThe files already have the parts in the right position and separated by colour. They use about 44 hours and 1129 g of filament. Six parts need supports; the parts table shows which ones and where.
3
Prepare the power and servos on the workbenchBefore closing any part, you solder the power wires, adjust both voltages with a multimeter, prepare the Raspberry Pi cables, and centre the servos with a program ready to use on the Arduino.
4
Set the threaded inserts and assemble18 brass threaded inserts with the soldering iron, then the chassis with the motors and boards, the body on top, the rear door, and finally the head. The robot charges via USB-C without being opened.

Glossary

Words used on this page

Electronics and 3D printing terms used in this guide, explained in one or two sentences each.

GPIO
The Raspberry Pi's 40-pin row, where the power and signal wires connect. In this guide, "pin 32" is the pin's position in the row; "GPIO12" is the name of the signal that comes out of it.
UBEC
A small converter used in model aircraft that lowers the battery voltage to a fixed value. Here, 6 V for the servos.
BMS
Lithium battery protection board. It disconnects the battery before it discharges too much, overcharges, or shorts out.
2S
Two lithium cells connected in series, one after the other. Their voltages add up: from 6 V (empty) to 8.4 V (full).
Horn
The plastic piece that fits onto the servo shaft and turns with it. The 2 large servos use the round, disc-shaped horn; the micro servos use the 1-arm horn.
Heat-set threaded insert
A brass bushing with an internal thread. It goes into the hole in the plastic when pushed in with a soldering iron, creating a metal thread.
NC
Unconfirmed: a measurement from a store listing that has not yet been checked against the part in hand.
Slicer
The software that turns the 3D file into the layers the printer prints. Bambu Lab's slicer is called Bambu Studio.
SSH
A way to use the Raspberry Pi over the network: you type commands in your computer's terminal, and they run on the Pi without connecting a keyboard or monitor to it.
UART (serial)
A simple data connection: one wire to send (TX) and one to receive (RX). The Raspberry Pi sends commands to the Arduino through it.
SEL jumper
A small plastic cap over 2 Sensor Shield pins, next to the blue screw terminal, marked SEL. When installed, it connects the Arduino's 5 V to the servo connectors. When removed, the servos receive only the power supplied through the blue screw terminal.
Screw terminal
A screw connector: the wire goes into the hole and the screw tightens it. On the Sensor Shield, it is the blue block marked GND and VCC.
Sketch
The name Arduino gives to a program. You open the file in Arduino IDE and upload it to the board with the USB cable.
Shield
A board that fits on top of the Arduino, onto its pins, and adds connectors. Here, the Sensor Shield provides the servo connectors.
AWG
A measure of wire thickness. The lower the number, the thicker the wire: 20 AWG is thicker than 22 AWG.
GND
The circuit’s negative, also called ground. All the robot’s negative connections are connected to each other.
Dupont
A thin wire that comes with a plug-in connector on each end, sized to fit the pins on the boards. Also called a jumper.
Step-down
A converter that lowers the voltage. Here, the LM2596 lowers the battery voltage to 5.1 V.
Heat-shrink tubing
A small plastic tube that shrinks with heat. Slip it over the solder joint and heat it: it tightens and insulates.
H-bridge (L298N)
Board that turns the wheel motors on and off and reverses them at the Arduino's command. It's also called a motor driver.
Micro servo
Small 9-gram servo (SG90 or MG90S). It opens the mouth here.
Pan and tilt
The head's two movements: pan means turning side to side; tilt means angling up and down.
ToF
Sensor that measures distance by timing how long light takes to travel out and back. It's part of phase 2.
Supports (for printing)
Extra material the slicer prints under a part that would otherwise start in midair. You remove it after printing. Tree supports grow like thin branches and are easier to remove.

Printing

How to print

Bambu Lab A1, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer. PETG for load-bearing parts, PLA for parts that only cover something.

Suggestion for the A1, not yet tested with these parts: use the textured PEI plate for both materials. In Bambu Studio, choose the Generic PLA profile for PLA and Generic PETG for PETG, and keep the temperatures set by the profile. Typical values to check: PLA with the nozzle at 220 °C and the build plate at 55–65 °C; PETG with the nozzle at 250–255 °C and the build plate at 70–80 °C. Bambu’s filament guide lists 240 to 270 °C for the PETG nozzle (source).

1

Print the test piece first

It is on build plate 06. Test one threaded insert in each of the 3 holes (3.9, 4.0, and 4.1 mm), an M3 screw in the through-hole and another in the hole for threading directly into the plastic, the micro servo in its window, and the motor shaft in its hole. The dimple marks the size used by the project. The coupon's 3 pin sockets are not used in this robot. The MG996R servo fit is not tested on the coupon: check it in the tilt cradle, which is printed on one of the first build plates. The coupon prints in PLA, and some of the inserts go in PETG parts: print a second coupon in PETG. It is not on any build plate or included in the totals: load comum-teste-encaixe.stl separately, using PETG, and allow about 15 g and 38 extra minutes. If another hole fits better than the one marked by the dimple, you do not need to reprint the parts: in Bambu Studio, the X-Y hole compensation option (X-Y hole compensation; Quality tab, Precision group, with advanced mode enabled) opens or closes all holes in the part: a positive value opens them, a negative value closes them (source: Bambu Studio code). The source does not say whether the value applies per side or to the diameter: start with 0.05 mm and print the coupon again. Let the coupon decide. If you edit the code, you can change the value in comum/cad/parametros.py (the FOLGA block) and regenerate the parts.

Assembly

How to assemble

First, four workbench prep steps with the parts still loose: all the soldering happens here. Then assembly, in the same order the checker tested: each part goes in from the indicated side without hitting anything already assembled.

Before assembly: 4 workbench prep steps

These prep steps come first because it’s much easier to solder, measure, and adjust the loose parts on the workbench than inside the body. All soldering for the robot is done here; during assembly, you only use screw terminals and plug-in connections. Prep steps 1, 2, and 3 are electrical and have not been bench-tested: measure every connection with a multimeter before turning on the switch. The sequence matches the wiring file, step by step.

1

Solder the power wires and adjust both voltages

You assemble the wiring harness by following the wiring diagram, with the printed chassis beside you. Don’t connect the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, motor driver, or servos yet.

🔰 Never soldered before? Read this before turning on the iron

  • Practice first. Make about ten wire splices using scrap wire before touching the robot's parts. Wear safety glasses and solder in a well-ventilated area: the iron tip gets hotter than 300 °C.
  • Tin both parts. Melt a little solder onto the stripped wire and a little onto the terminal, separately. Then touch them together and heat them: they join in 2 or 3 seconds.
  • Heat the joint, not the solder. Touch the iron to the wire and terminal at the same time, and feed the solder to the hot joint, not to the iron tip.
  • A good solder joint is smooth and wraps around the wire. Dull, grainy, or ball-shaped solder is a cold joint: it makes poor contact. Heat it again until it flows.
  • Check with a multimeter. Set it to continuity mode, the one that beeps, and test each joint. Before putting the cells in the holder, check that the positive and negative terminals are not connected.
  • Never solder directly to an 18650 cell. Heat damages the cell, and it can leak or catch fire. On this robot, solder the battery connection to the holder's metal strip, with the cells out of the holder. Another option is to ask a battery shop for a pre-soldered pack with wires: in that case, first check that it fits where the holder goes.
  • Get help with the battery. If you are under 18 or don't feel safe, ask an adult or an electronics technician to do the battery soldering.

Tutorial in Portuguese, with photos: How to solder: learn to make a perfect solder joint (Fazedores blog, by Manoel Lemos).

  1. Solder the 3rd wire (yellow) to the metal strip joining the two cells, with the cells out of the holder.
  2. Solder 2 15 cm red wires to the switch's 2 terminals. Pass the free ends through the chassis floor cutout, from bottom to top, and fit the switch into the cutout from underneath. Do this BEFORE soldering the switch to the rest: once connected to the wiring harness, it won't fit through the 13.5 × 9 mm cutout.
  3. If the motors came without wires, solder 2 20 cm wires (one red and one black, 22 AWG) to the 2 terminals on each motor.
  4. With the chassis beside you as a guide for wire length, connect the battery holder, fuse, and protection board according to the wiring table. Leave about 8 cm of slack in the holder wires so it can be pulled out from the rear when replacing the cells.
  5. The charger sits OUTSIDE the rear door, inside the tail, and its board does not fit through the door's 7 mm hole. So: solder 3 wires, 20 cm long (red, yellow, and black), to the protection board, pass the free ends through the door hole from inside to outside, and only then solder them to the charger. From this point on, the door is attached to the wiring harness by these 3 wires.
  6. Solder one switch terminal to the protection board's positive output. At the other terminal, join 3 red wires: the LM2596 IN+ input, the UBEC red input wire, and a 15 cm wire that goes to the driver's motor power terminal. At the protection board's negative output, join 3 black wires: the LM2596 IN− input, the UBEC black input wire, and a 15 cm wire for the driver's GND terminal. Insulate each splice with heat-shrink tubing.
  7. At the LM2596 output, solder 2 short wires and tighten their other ends in the terminal block: red in terminal 1, black in terminal 2. Don't connect anything else to the outputs.
  8. Put the cells in the holder, checking which side is positive, and turn on the switch.
  9. With the multimeter connected to the terminal block, turn the LM2596 adjustment screw with the screwdriver until it reads 5.1 V. It takes several turns.
  10. Measure the UBEC output: it should read 6 V. If it reads 5 V, move its jumper to 6 V.
  11. Turn off the switch.
2

Upload the program to the Arduino and set the 3 servos to 90°

The program is the sketch centrar_servos.ino. It moves all 3 servos to the middle point and holds them there, with the motors off.

  1. Open the centrar_servos.ino sketch in Arduino IDE. Connect the Arduino Uno to the computer with the USB cable, without the shield or battery.
  2. From the Tools menu, select the Arduino Uno board and the port it appeared on. Click Upload.
  3. Unplug the USB cable, fit the Sensor Shield onto the Uno, and remove the shield’s SEL jumper: it is the small plastic cap over 2 pins, next to the blue screw terminal.
  4. Reconnect the USB cable just to take a measurement with the multimeter. The shield's COM connector has 4 upright pins labeled TX, RX, −, and +. You should get about 5 V between + and −. On the D9 connector, you should get 0 V between the V pin and the G pin.
  5. If the D9 V pin reads 5 V, the SEL jumper is still in place. If the COM + pin does not read 5 V, your shield differs from the store photo: look for another pin labeled + or 5V that reads 5 V with SEL removed. A wrong pin here sends the servos' 6 V to the Raspberry Pi.
  6. Unplug the USB cable.
  7. The UBEC output has a 3-pin servo plug: cut it off and strip the wires. Red is positive and black (or brown) is negative. If there’s a third wire, white or orange, insulate its end with heat-shrink tubing. Clamp the red wire into VCC and the black wire into GND on the blue screw terminal. The 1000 µF capacitor goes into the same terminal, with its longer lead in VCC.
  8. Take 2 jumper wires, cut off the connector at one end of each, and strip the wires. Tighten one in terminal 1 of the terminal block, on the side opposite the converter, and plug its connector into the + pin of the COM connector. Connect the other from terminal 2 to the − pin of the same connector. Leave the TX and RX pins next to it empty: 5 V on either one reaches the Raspberry Pi through the serial wire.
  9. Connect the pan servo to D9, the tilt servo to D10, and the mouth servo to D11: brown or black wire to G, red to V, orange or yellow to S.
  10. Turn on the switch: the 3 servos move to the centre (90°), and the Uno LED flashes once per second.
  11. Turn off the switch. From now on, do not turn the servo shafts by hand.
  12. Undo the test connections for assembly: remove the servos from the shield, disconnect the UBEC wires and capacitor from the blue terminal, and remove the 2 jumpers from the COM connector. Label each servo cable with tape and the pin name. Remove the 2 cells from the battery holder: they go back in during assembly, at the battery step.

Don’t power the servos through USB: it supplies 0.5 A, and an MG996R servo needs more when starting up. Never connect the computer’s USB cable while the battery is on. To upload the program again later, pull out the Arduino tray.

3

Prepare the Raspberry Pi cables, resistor divider, and driver wires

The Raspberry Pi and Arduino sit in trays, one above the other. They communicate through 3 wires, and the Pi gets power through 4 wires on the pins. Here you prepare and label those cables, assemble the 3-resistor divider that protects the Pi from the Arduino’s 5 V, and set aside the motor driver wires. After this prep step, there’s no more soldering.

  1. Set aside 3 female-to-female jumper wires for the serial connection: one green, one blue, and one black (or mark 3 wires of another color with tape). Leave the green and black wires intact. On the blue wire, cut off the connector at one end and strip 6 mm: this end goes into a terminal.
  2. Now make the divider. Solder 2 1 kΩ resistors together, leg to leg, and cover the joint with heat-shrink tubing. This pair adds up to 2 kΩ.
  3. Cut a jumper in half. Solder the cut end of one half to a leg of the 3rd resistor and cover it with heat-shrink tubing. The female connector on this half goes to D1.
  4. On the terminal block, on the converter side, tighten the free leg of the 3rd resistor together with one end of the 2 kΩ pair in terminal 3. Tighten the other end of the pair in terminal 2, together with the black wire already there. Bend the end of each leg before tightening, so the screw grips it.
  5. Check with the multimeter in ohms: between the D1 jumper’s female connector and terminal 3, about 1 kΩ; between terminal 3 and terminal 2, about 2 kΩ.
  6. Set aside 4 more jumper wires for Raspberry Pi power, 2 red and 2 black (or mark them with tape). Cut off the connector at one end of each and strip 6 mm. Tighten the 2 red wires together in terminal 1 of the terminal block, on the side opposite the converter, and the 2 black wires together in terminal 2.
  7. Cut a 15 cm red wire (22 AWG) and tighten one end in terminal 1 of the terminal block, together with the 2 red jumper wires: the other end goes to the driver's +5V terminal during assembly.
  8. Find the pins on the Raspberry Pi: pin 1 is in the corner next to the microSD card, on the inner row; pin 40 is in the opposite corner, on the outer row. Odd-numbered pins are on the inner row and even-numbered pins are on the outer row. Pins 2, 4, and 6 are the first 3 on the outer row; pin 9 is the 5th on the inner row; pins 32 and 34 are the 16th and 17th on the outer row; pin 33 is the 17th on the inner row.
  9. Label each connector with tape and the pin number: 2 and 4 on the red wires, 6 and 9 on the black power wires, 32 on the green wire, 33 on the blue wire, and 34 on the black serial wire. A red wire on the wrong pin sends 5 V to a signal pin and burns out the Raspberry Pi.
  10. Set aside 6 intact female-to-female jumper wires for the driver signals and label the ends: ENA-D5, IN1-D4, IN2-D7, IN3-D8, IN4-D12, and ENB-D6.
  11. Put the cables aside: during assembly, they connect to the Raspberry Pi, shield, and driver using terminals or connectors, with no soldering. Attention: on the shield's 3-pin connectors, the serial and driver wires use only the S pin (and the G pin for the black serial wire). The middle pin (V) must always stay empty: with the SEL jumper removed, it carries the servos' 6 V, and 6 V on one of these wires will burn out the Raspberry Pi or driver.

Where to find pin 1 and why the pin number is not the GPIO number: Raspberry Pi documentation. On a powered-on Pi, the pinout command draws the pin map. There is about 19 mm left above the Pi up to the top tray (measurement unconfirmed): bend the wires just above the connectors.

4

Flash the microSD card and connect the Raspberry Pi serial

The card is flashed on your computer with a microSD card reader. Then you connect to the Raspberry Pi over the network using SSH: you type commands on your computer, and they run on the Pi.

  1. Install Raspberry Pi Imager, the free Raspberry Pi program, on your computer, and put the card in the reader.
  2. In Imager, choose your Pi model, the Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit) operating system, and the card.
  3. Before flashing, open Imager’s settings: give the Pi a name (for example, robo), create a username and password, enter your Wi-Fi name and password, and enable SSH. Flash the card.
  4. Put the card in the Raspberry Pi and power it on at the workbench, outside the robot, using a 5 V, 3 A USB-C power supply. Wait about 2 minutes.
  5. On your computer, open the terminal: on Windows, use the PowerShell program; on Mac and Linux, use Terminal.
  6. Type ssh usuario@robo.local, replacing usuario and robo with the names you created. Answer yes the first time and enter the password.
  7. If robo.local doesn’t respond, open your router’s page, find the Pi in the list of connected devices, and use its number (the IP address), for example ssh usuario@192.168.0.50.
  8. When the terminal shows the Pi’s name, you’re in. Enable the serial port: type sudo nano /boot/firmware/config.txt, go to the end of the file, and add a line: dtoverlay=uart5 on the Raspberry Pi 4, or dtoverlay=uart4-pi5 on the Raspberry Pi 5. Save with Ctrl+O and Enter, then exit with Ctrl+X.
  9. Type sudo poweroff, wait for the Pi’s green LED to stop blinking, and unplug the power supply. Inside the robot, the Raspberry Pi no longer uses the USB-C port: it gets 5.1 V through the pins.

Sources: Raspberry Pi documentation on GitHub (SSH and how to find the Pi's address). The serial line comes from the Raspberry Pi firmware overlay list; after restarting, the port name appears with ls /dev/ttyAMA*. Not bench-tested.

Power and wiring

Where each wire goes

Two 18650 batteries in series provide 6 to 8.4 V. Three power lines come from them: 5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, and driver logic; 6 V for the servos; and battery voltage for the wheel motors.

⚠️ Lithium battery safety

  • None of this has been bench-tested. The wiring diagram is based on the component datasheets. Check each connection with a multimeter before turning on the switch.
  • Polarity: red to positive, black to negative. Measure before each new connection. Reversed wires burn out the board immediately.
  • Adjust before connecting the load: set the LM2596 to 5.1 V and the UBEC to 6 V, measured with a multimeter, with the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, and servos disconnected.
  • A short circuit can start a fire: solder with the cells OUT of the holder and insulate each splice with heat-shrink tubing. The 3 A fuse goes right after the holder.
  • Matching cells: use two new 18650 cells from the same brand and model. Take dented, swollen, or hot cells to a battery collection point, not the robot.
  • First charge: stay nearby, with the robot on a surface that won’t burn.
2 × 18650 in series (2S pack)→ fuse + protection board + switch→ LM2596: 5.1 V for the boards · UBEC: 6 V for the servos · L298N: wheel motors

5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi and Arduino

The LM2596 converter keeps the output at 5 V until the battery drops to about 6.5 V. Its output goes to a terminal block inside the body. From there, 4 jumpers go to the Raspberry Pi's 5 V and GND pins, one pair goes to the + and − pins of the Sensor Shield's COM connector, and one wire goes to the driver's +5V terminal. The board can handle about 2 A continuously without a heatsink (typical value, NC): use the Pi 4, or the Pi 5 without USB accessories. If the Pi reports undervoltage (the vcgencmd get_throttled command returns something other than throttled=0x0), shorten these wires and check the 5.1 V.

6 V for the servos

The UBEC supplies 3 A continuously to the 3 servos through the Sensor Shield's blue screw terminal, with the SEL jumper removed. With the jumper in place, the 6 V would reach the Arduino's 5 V line. A 1000 µF capacitor on the screw terminal handles the startup surge. When all servos stall at once, they draw more than 3 A: the phase 2 program must stop them before they reach the end of their travel.

Charging via USB-C

The balanced charger stands upright inside the tail, with its opening facing down: the plug goes in under the tail. It accepts a USB-C power supply up to 15 W and needs a third wire soldered to the point joining the two cells. It connects before the switch, so the robot charges while switched off.

Wheel motors and communication between the boards

The L298N motor driver receives battery voltage and connects the 2 motors as directed by the Arduino, through 6 signal wires. The Raspberry Pi and Arduino communicate over a 3-wire serial connection (UART): the Pi transmits on pin 32 and receives on pin 33. These numbers are the pin positions in the row of 40, counted from the corner of the microSD card; they are not the GPIO numbers. The Arduino runs at 5 V and the Pi at 3.3 V: 3 1 kΩ resistors, attached to the terminal block, lower the voltage on the wire going from the Arduino to the Pi. On the Pi system, enable serial with one line in the configuration file: dtoverlay=uart5 on the Pi 4 or dtoverlay=uart4-pi5 on the Pi 5 (prep step 4).

Summary of the 11 tests, one per line

  • 1. count: The number of parts is as expected. ✓ passed
  • 2. mesh: Each file is a single, closed solid. ✓ passed
  • 3. build plate: Each part fits within 256 × 256 × 256 mm in the print orientation. ✓ passed
  • 4. wall: The shells have walls 2 mm thick or more. ✓ passed
  • 5. interference: No part occupies the same space as another part or a component (servo, motor, board, battery), with the moving parts at the ends of their travel. ✓ passed
  • 6. interference negative test: The test itself is tested: one part is deliberately put out of place, and test 5 must detect it. ✓ passed
  • 7. loose part: Every assembled part touches another: none is floating. ✓ passed
  • 8. balance: The centre of gravity falls within the supports, with a margin of 10 mm or more, in every position. ✓ passed
  • 9. path: Each part goes in and comes out through the side indicated in the assembly, without hitting anything already assembled. ✓ passed
  • 10. printability: A part declared support-free has no unsupported area above the limit. ✓ passed
  • 11. printability negative test: One part is deliberately turned upside down, and test 10 must detect it. ✓ passed

✅ What the tests guarantee

  • Each STL is a closed mesh and fits on the 256 mm build plate.
  • No part intersects another part or a component in any of the 8 head and mouth positions.
  • Each part can be inserted and removed from the side specified in the assembly instructions.
  • The robot does not tip over with the head at either extreme.
  • The shell has walls at least 2 mm thick.
  • 23 of the 29 parts print without supports; the 6 that need supports are listed with their locations.

⚠️ What only printing can tell you

  • The actual clearances on your printer: that's why the test coupon comes first.
  • The measurements marked NC for purchased components.
  • The actual print time and weight: the numbers are estimates without a slicer.
  • The strength of the fork and neck cradle in use.
  • How the supports come out of the skull and body.
  • The electrical system: the wiring diagram is based on component datasheets and has not been bench-tested.
