In short

The square robot is a 20 cm desktop robot you can print at home and assemble. Its head is a cube with a 3.5-inch screen for a face; it rotates almost a full turn and tilts up and down. Inside are a Raspberry Pi, an Arduino, two servos, and a battery that charges via USB-C. You need a 3D printer with a 256 mm build plate, the parts on the shopping list, and a soldering iron. This is phase 1: the body and fittings. Programming (voice, camera, AI) comes in phase 2.

All images on this page are renders of the actual 3D model. Nothing has been printed yet: you will be the first to print it, starting with the test piece. The project's other robots are at general robot guide

The robot

What will sit on your desk

A cube with a face on top of a low base. No screws are visible from the front: everything is secured from below or behind.

Inside

The screen, Raspberry Pi, and tilt servo are inside the head. The Arduino, battery, power converters, and neck rotation servo are inside the base. The cables run up through the hollow neck.

What supports the weight

The load-bearing parts are made of PETG: the base, cover, neck, gears, fork, and head frame. The white cup and dark face panel are made of PLA and simply fit over the robot.

↔️ Rotates ±170°

A standard 180° servo moves two printed gears with 44 and 22 teeth. The neck rotates twice as far as the servo: almost a full turn.

↕️ Tilts from −25° to +30°

The tilt servo is hidden inside the head. From the outside, you only see the slim neck.

🙂 Face on the screen

The 3.5-inch screen plugs directly into the Raspberry Pi. The program draws the eyes and mouth on it; that’s phase 2.

The project checker tests the balance with the head in 10 positions. The result is at the end of this page.

The process

From file to robot in 4 steps

The order matters: the first print takes less than an hour and helps you avoid losing a whole day of printer time.

1
Print the test pieceA small coupon with the servo opening and insert and screw holes at three clearances. You’ll find out which clearance works with your printer before using up filament.
2
Print the 7 build platesThe files already have the parts laid flat and sorted by color. It takes about 28 hours and 735 g of filament, with no supports on any part.
3
Set the inserts and assembleInstall 16 brass inserts with a soldering iron, then assemble the base, the head on the workbench, and finally the head onto the base.
4
Connect the powerBattery, protection board, 5 V converter for the Raspberry Pi, and 6 V converter for the servos. The robot charges via USB-C without being opened.

Each step is explained in detail in the technical section just below. go to the step-by-step ↓

Bambu Lab A1, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer. PETG for load-bearing parts, PLA for parts that only cover something.

Power

How power is distributed

Two 18650 batteries in series provide 6 to 8.4 V. From there, two separate lines branch off: one for the brain, the other for the motors.

2 × 18650 in series (2S pack)→ protection board + switch→ LM2596: 5 V for the Raspberry Pi · UBEC: 6 V for the 2 servos

5 V for the Raspberry Pi

The LM2596 converter maintains 5 V until the battery drops to about 6.5 V. Set the output to 5.1 V with a multimeter before connecting the Pi. It delivers up to 3 A: use the Pi 4, or the Pi 5 without power-hungry USB accessories.

6 V for the servos

The UBEC delivers 3 A continuously for the two MG996R servos. A 1000 µF capacitor on the output handles the startup surge. The program must not let both servos push against their end stops at the same time.

Charging via USB-C

The balance charger sits with its port in a window behind the base. It needs a third wire, soldered to the point joining the two cells. The protection board disconnects the battery before it is deeply discharged.

What exists today is the location for each module and the decision about which ones to use. The wire-by-wire diagram and electrical test will come after the first assembly. Check the polarity with a multimeter before connecting any board: a shorted lithium battery can catch fire.

✅ What the tests guarantee

  • Each STL is a closed mesh and fits on the 256 mm build plate.
  • No part passes through another part or component in 10 head positions.
  • Each part goes in and comes out through the side indicated in the assembly instructions.
  • The robot doesn’t tip over with the head at either extreme.
  • No part needs supports in the file’s print position.

⚠️ What only printing can tell you

  • The actual clearances on your printer: that’s why the test part comes first.
  • The dimensions marked NC on purchased components.
  • The actual time and weight: these numbers are estimates made without a slicer.
  • The strength of the gears and fork in use.
  • The electrical side: no wires have been connected yet.
