In short

The square robot is a 20 cm desktop robot that you can print at home and assemble. Its head is a cube with a 3.5-inch screen on its face; it rotates almost all the way around and tilts up and down. Inside are a Raspberry Pi, an Arduino, two servos, and a battery that charges via USB-C. You need a 3D printer with a 256 mm build plate, the parts on the shopping list, and a soldering iron. This page covers phase 1: the body assembled, the wires connected, and both servos centered. Programming (screen face, voice, camera, AI) is phase 2 and has not been done yet.

Inside

The screen, Raspberry Pi, and tilt servo are inside the head. The Arduino, battery, power converters, and neck rotation servo are inside the base. The cables run up through the hollow neck.

What supports the weight

The parts that bear force are made of PETG: base, lid, neck, gears, fork, and head frame. The white head shell and dark face panel are made of PLA and only cover the robot.

↔️ Rotates ±170°

A standard 180° servo moves two printed gears with 44 and 22 teeth. The neck rotates twice as far as the servo: almost a full turn.

↕️ Tilts from −25° to +30°

The tilt servo is hidden inside the head. From the outside, you only see the slim neck.

🙂 Face on the screen

The 3.5-inch screen plugs directly into the Raspberry Pi. The program draws the eyes and mouth on it; that’s phase 2.

The project checker tests the balance with the head in 10 positions. The result is at the end of this page.

⚠️ Risks to consider before buying

What hasn't been tested yet. Read before spending money.

  • Nothing has been printed or assembled yet. The electrical section of this page has not been bench-tested: it was based on the component datasheets.
  • The 3MF files for the build plates have never been opened in Bambu Studio. If one won’t open, use the STL files.
  • The USB-C plug that powers the Raspberry Pi is not in the 3D model. There may not be room for it next to the Pi inside the head.
  • Raspberry Pi pins 31 to 34 are under the screen board. The clearance there (17.5 mm in the model) is unconfirmed: if the low-profile connector doesn't fit, the 3 serial wires will have to be soldered.
  • The test coupon does not check the MG996R servo window or the M5 screw thread. Check the servo window in the head frame (build plate 01). The M5 thread is in the fork arm (build plate 03). Both are printed before the long print jobs.
  • With the Arduino screwed to the base, the USB cable won't fit: there is about 10 mm of clearance to the wall. To reprogram it, you need to remove its 4 screws.
  • The fuse holder, terminal block, and 3 serial resistors have no modeled mounting spot: they hang loose on the wires, insulated with heat-shrink tubing.
  • Measurements marked NC come from store listings. Measure your part before printing the section that touches it.
  • The path for the 8 wires through the fork hole and the slot in the bottom of the head shell has never been tested with printed parts.
  • Heat: the Raspberry Pi 4 is enclosed in the head, which is made of PLA and has no ventilation opening. PLA softens near 60 °C, and the Pi throttles starting at 80 °C. Attach the heatsinks and measure with the vcgencmd measure_temp command. If it goes above 70 °C, print the head shell in PETG or open ventilation holes at the back.

The process

From file to robot in 4 steps

The order matters: the first print takes less than an hour and helps you avoid losing a whole day of printer time.

1
Print the test pieceA small test coupon with threaded insert holes in three sizes and screw holes. You can find out which size works with your printer before using up filament.
2
Print the 7 build platesThe files already have the parts laid flat and sorted by color. It takes about 28 hours and 735 g of filament, with no supports on any part.
3
Prepare the power and servos on the workbenchBefore closing any part, you solder the power wires and the serial cable, adjust both voltages with the multimeter, and center both servos with a ready-to-use program for Arduino.
4
Set the threaded inserts and assembleInstall 16 brass threaded inserts with the soldering iron, then assemble the base, the head on the workbench, and finally the head onto the base. The robot charges via USB-C, without opening it.

Glossary

Words used on this page

Electronics and 3D printing terms used in this guide, explained in one or two sentences each.

GPIO
The row of 40 pins on the Raspberry Pi, where the screen and signal wires connect. In this guide, "pin 32" is the pin’s position in the row; "GPIO12" is the name of the signal that comes out of it.
UBEC
A small converter used in model aircraft that lowers the battery voltage to a fixed value. Here, 6 V for the servos.
BMS
Lithium battery protection board. It disconnects the battery before it discharges too much, overcharges, or shorts out.
2S
Two lithium cells connected in series, one after the other. Their voltages add up: from 6 V (empty) to 8.4 V (full).
Horn
The plastic part that fits onto the servo shaft and turns with it. This robot uses the round, disk-shaped horn.
Heat-set threaded insert
A brass bushing with an internal thread. It goes into the hole in the plastic when pushed in with a soldering iron, creating a metal thread.
NC
Unconfirmed: a measurement from a store listing that has not yet been checked against the part in hand.
Slicer
The software that turns the 3D file into the layers the printer prints. Bambu Lab's slicer is called Bambu Studio.
SSH
A way to use the Raspberry Pi over the network: you type commands in your computer's terminal, and they run on the Pi without connecting a keyboard or monitor to it.
SPI
A data connection that uses a few wires and a clock to keep things in step. The Raspberry Pi sends the image to the screen through it.
UART (serial)
A simple data connection: one wire to send (TX) and one to receive (RX). The Raspberry Pi sends commands to the Arduino through it.
SEL jumper
A small plastic cap over 2 Sensor Shield pins, next to the blue screw terminal, marked SEL. When installed, it connects the Arduino's 5 V to the servo connectors. When removed, the servos receive only the power supplied through the blue screw terminal.
Screw terminal
A screw connector: the wire goes into the hole and the screw tightens it. On the Sensor Shield, it is the blue block marked GND and VCC.
Sketch
The name Arduino gives to a program. You open the file in Arduino IDE and upload it to the board with the USB cable.
Shield
A board that fits on top of the Arduino, onto its pins, and adds connectors. Here, the Sensor Shield provides the servo connectors.
AWG
A measure of wire thickness. The lower the number, the thicker the wire: 20 AWG is thicker than 22 AWG.
GND
The circuit’s negative, also called ground. All the robot’s negative connections are connected to each other.
Dupont
A thin wire that comes with a plug-in connector on each end, sized to fit the pins on the boards. Also called a jumper.
Step-down
A converter that lowers the voltage. Here, the LM2596 lowers the battery voltage to 5.1 V.
Heat-shrink tubing
A small plastic tube that shrinks with heat. Slip it over the solder joint and heat it: it tightens and insulates.

Printing

How to print

Bambu Lab A1, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer. PETG for load-bearing parts, PLA for parts that only cover something.

Suggestion for the A1, not yet tested with these parts: use the textured PEI plate for both materials. In Bambu Studio, choose the Generic PLA profile for PLA and Generic PETG for PETG, and keep the temperatures set by the profile. Typical values to check: PLA with the nozzle at 220 °C and the build plate at 55–65 °C; PETG with the nozzle at 250–255 °C and the build plate at 70–80 °C. Bambu’s filament guide lists 240 to 270 °C for the PETG nozzle (source).

1

Print the test piece first

It is on build plate 06, together with the 4 Raspberry Pi spacers. Test one threaded insert in each of the 3 holes (3.9, 4.0, and 4.1 mm), one M3 screw in the through-hole, and another in the hole for threading directly into the plastic. The dimple marks the size used by the project. The servo window and the coupon sockets are for the other robots in the project: check the MG996R servo fit in the head frame (build plate 01) and the M5 screw thread in the fork (build plate 03). The coupon is printed in PLA, and most inserts go into PETG parts: print a second coupon in PETG. It is not on any build plate or included in the totals: load the file comum-teste-encaixe.stl separately, using PETG, and allow about 15 g and 38 extra minutes. If another hole works better than the one marked by the dimple, you do not need to remake the parts: in Bambu Studio, the X-Y hole compensation option (X-Y hole compensation; Quality tab, Precision group, with advanced mode enabled) opens or closes all the holes in the part: a positive value opens them, a negative value closes them (source: Bambu Studio code). The source does not say whether the value applies per side or to the diameter: start with 0.05 mm and print the coupon again. Let the coupon decide. If you work with the code, you can change the value in comum/cad/parametros.py (the FOLGA block) and generate the parts again.

Assembly

How to assemble

First, four workbench prep steps with the parts still loose: all the soldering happens here. Then assembly, in the same order the checker tested: each part goes in from the indicated side without hitting anything already assembled.

Before assembly: 4 workbench prep steps

These come first because, once the robot is closed, you can no longer reach the converter adjustment, the Arduino USB port, or the Raspberry Pi pins and card. All soldering on the robot is done here; assembly only uses screw terminals and fittings. Prep steps 1, 2, and 3 are electrical and have not been bench-tested: measure each connection with the multimeter before turning on the switch. The sequence is the same as in the wiring file, step by step.

1

Solder the power wires and adjust both voltages

Assemble the wiring harness following the wiring diagram, with the printed base beside you. Do not connect the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, or servos yet.

  1. Solder the 3rd wire (yellow) to the metal strip joining the two cells, with the cells out of the holder.
  2. Solder 2 red 10 cm wires to the 2 switch terminals. Pass the free ends through the cutout in the base, from the outside in, and fit the switch into the cutout. Do this BEFORE soldering the switch to the rest of the wiring: once connected to the wiring harness, it won’t fit through the 13.5 × 9 mm cutout.
  3. With the base beside you as a guide for wire lengths, connect the holder, fuse, protection board, and charger according to the wiring table. Leave about 8 cm of slack in the holder wires so you can pull it out through the rear door when it’s time to replace the cells.
  4. Solder one end of the switch to the protection board’s positive output. Solder the other end to the LM2596 IN+ input and the UBEC red input wire. Connect the negatives of both inputs to the protection board’s negative output.
  5. At the LM2596 output, solder 2 short 20 AWG wires and tighten their other ends in the terminal block: red in screw terminal 1, black in screw terminal 2. Do not connect anything else to the outputs.
  6. Put the cells in the holder, checking which side is positive, and turn on the switch.
  7. With the multimeter connected to the terminal block, turn the LM2596 adjustment screw with the screwdriver until it reads 5.1 V. It takes several turns.
  8. Measure the UBEC output: it should read 6 V. If it reads 5 V, move its jumper to 6 V.
  9. Turn off the switch.
2

Upload the program to the Arduino and set both servos to 90°

The program is the sketch centrar_servos.ino. It moves both servos to the middle point and keeps them there.

  1. Open the centrar_servos.ino sketch in Arduino IDE. Connect the Arduino Uno to the computer with the USB cable, without the shield or battery.
  2. From the Tools menu, select the Arduino Uno board and the port it appeared on. Click Upload.
  3. Unplug the USB cable, fit the Sensor Shield onto the Uno, and remove the shield’s SEL jumper: it is the small plastic cap over 2 pins, next to the blue screw terminal.
  4. Reconnect the USB cable just to take a measurement with the multimeter. The shield's COM connector has 4 upright pins labeled TX, RX, −, and +. You should get about 5 V between + and −. On the D9 connector, you should get 0 V between the V pin and the G pin.
  5. If the D9 V pin reads 5 V, the SEL jumper is still in place. If the COM + pin does not read 5 V, your shield differs from the store photo: look for another pin labeled + or 5V that reads 5 V with SEL removed. A wrong pin here sends the servos' 6 V to the Raspberry Pi.
  6. Unplug the USB cable.
  7. The UBEC output has a 3-pin servo plug: cut it off and strip the wires. Red is positive and black (or brown) is negative. If there’s a third wire, white or orange, insulate its end with heat-shrink tubing. Clamp the red wire into VCC and the black wire into GND on the blue screw terminal. The 1000 µF capacitor goes into the same terminal, with its longer lead in VCC.
  8. Take 2 40 cm jumper wires, cut the connector off one end of each, and strip the wires. Clamp one in terminal 1 of the terminal block, opposite the converter, and plug its connector into the + pin on the COM connector. Connect the other from terminal 2 to the − pin on the same connector. Leave the TX and RX pins beside it empty: 5 V on one of them reaches the Raspberry Pi through the serial wire.
  9. Connect the pan servo to D9 and the tilt servo to D10: brown or black wire to G, red to V, orange or yellow to S.
  10. Turn on the switch: both servos move to the center (90°) and the Uno LED blinks once per second.
  11. Turn off the switch. From now on, do not turn the servo shafts by hand.
  12. Undo the test connections for assembly: remove the 2 servos from D9 and D10, disconnect the UBEC wires and capacitor from the blue screw terminal, and remove the 2 jumpers from the COM connector. If the shield covers the Uno mounting holes, remove it too. Take the 2 cells out of the holder: they go back in during assembly, at the battery step.

Do not power the MG996R servo through USB: it supplies 0.5 A, and the servo draws more when starting. Never connect the computer's USB cable while the battery is connected. Upload the sketch before screwing the Uno onto the base: once it is in place, the USB cable will not fit.

3

Make the serial cable and resistor divider

The Raspberry Pi goes in the head and the Arduino in the base. They communicate through 3 wires that run down the neck. Here you prepare these wires, with a low-profile connector at the Pi end, and assemble the 3-resistor divider that protects the Pi from the Arduino's 5 V. No more soldering is needed after this prep step.

  1. With the wire cutters, cut a 2 × 2 piece from the female pin header: 2 rows with 2 holes each. This is the low-profile connector that goes on Raspberry Pi pins 31 to 34.
  2. Set aside 3 40 cm jumpers: one green, one blue, and one black (or mark 3 jumpers of another color with tape). Cut the connector off ONE end of each and strip 3 mm.
  3. Find the pins on the Raspberry Pi: pin 1 is at the corner next to the microSD card, on the inner row; pin 40 is at the opposite corner, on the outer row. Odd-numbered pins are on the inner row and even-numbered pins are on the outer row. Pins 31 and 32 are the 16th pair; pins 33 and 34 are the 17th.
  4. Solder the 3 wire ends to the terminals of the 2 × 2 connector, with the wires lying flat and pointing to the side: green to the hole that will go on pin 32, blue to pin 33, black to pin 34. Leave the pin 31 terminal empty. Slide a piece of thin heat-shrink tubing over each solder joint.
  5. Mark with a pen the corner of the connector where the empty terminal is, the one for pin 31. When you plug it in, the mark goes on the inner row, facing the microSD card. To check: there are 2 pairs of empty pins (27 to 30) between the connector and the display, and 3 pairs (35 to 40) on the other side. If the connector is reversed, it swaps the wires around.
  6. On the blue wire, also cut off the connector at the other end and strip 6 mm: this end goes into a screw terminal. Leave the green and black wires with their female connectors, which fit onto the shield.
  7. Now make the divider. Solder 2 1 kΩ resistors together, leg to leg, and cover the joint with heat-shrink tubing. This pair adds up to 2 kΩ.
  8. Cut a jumper in half. Solder the cut end of one half to a leg of the 3rd resistor and cover it with heat-shrink tubing. The female connector on this half goes to D1.
  9. On the terminal block, on the converter side, tighten the free leg of the 3rd resistor together with one end of the 2 kΩ pair in terminal 3. Tighten the other end of the pair in terminal 2, together with the black wire already there. Bend the end of each leg before tightening, so the screw grips it.
  10. Check with the multimeter in ohms: between the D1 jumper’s female connector and terminal 3, about 1 kΩ; between terminal 3 and terminal 2, about 2 kΩ.
  11. Set the cable aside: the 2 × 2 connector plugs into the Raspberry Pi during assembly, before the display. At the base, the blue wire goes to terminal 3 on the terminal block, the green wire to the S pin of D0, and the black wire to the G pin of D0; use screw terminals or plug-in connections, no soldering. Attention: on the 3-pin connectors for D0 and D1, always leave the middle point (V) empty. With the SEL jumper removed, it carries the servos' 6 V, and 6 V on a serial wire will burn out the Raspberry Pi.

Where pin 1 is and why the pin number is not the GPIO number: Raspberry Pi documentation. On a powered-on Pi, the pinout command draws a map of the pins. Pins 31 to 34 are beneath the display board, where about 17.5 mm remains (unconfirmed measurement): that is why the connector is low-profile and the wires come out flat.

4

Flash the microSD card, install the screen driver, and connect the serial cable

The card is flashed on your computer with a microSD card reader. Then you connect to the Raspberry Pi over the network using SSH: you type commands on your computer, and they run on the Pi.

  1. Install Raspberry Pi Imager, the free Raspberry Pi program, on your computer, and put the card in the reader.
  2. In Imager, choose your Pi model, the Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit) operating system, and the card.
  3. Before flashing, open Imager’s settings: give the Pi a name (for example, robo), create a username and password, enter your Wi-Fi name and password, and enable SSH. Flash the card.
  4. Put the card in the Pi, fit the screen onto pins 1 to 26 just for this test, and power on the Pi with a 5 V, 3 A USB-C power supply. Wait about 2 minutes.
  5. On your computer, open the terminal: on Windows, use the PowerShell program; on Mac and Linux, use Terminal.
  6. Type ssh usuario@robo.local, replacing usuario and robo with the names you created. Answer yes the first time and enter the password.
  7. If robo.local doesn’t respond, open your router’s page, find the Pi in the list of connected devices, and use its number (the IP address), for example ssh usuario@192.168.0.50.
  8. When the terminal shows the Pi’s name, you’re inside it. Type the three driver commands, one per line, pressing Enter after each: git clone https://github.com/goodtft/LCD-show.git, then cd LCD-show, then sudo ./LCD35-show. The Pi needs internet access. It restarts on its own and the screen turns on.
  9. Connect again over SSH and enable the serial port AFTER the screen script, because it rewrites the configuration file. Type sudo nano /boot/firmware/config.txt, go to the end of the file, and add a line: dtoverlay=uart5 on Raspberry Pi 4, or dtoverlay=uart4-pi5 on Raspberry Pi 5. Save with Ctrl+O and Enter, then exit with Ctrl+X.
  10. Type sudo poweroff, wait for the Pi’s green LED to stop blinking, unplug the power supply, and detach the screen: you’ll put it back during assembly.

The display is the MPI3501 (ILI9486 controller, connected over SPI). Sources: MPI3501 page on lcdwiki (driver) and Raspberry Pi documentation on GitHub (SSH and how to find the Pi's address). Not bench-tested: the manufacturer lists tests up to the Raspberry Pi 3B+. Plan B, also not tested: instead of the script, add the line dtoverlay=piscreen,drm to the end of the /boot/firmware/config.txt file and restart (this is the official overlay for a 3.5-inch display with the same controller; the source does not guarantee that it works with the MPI3501). If none of this turns on the display, flash the card again with Raspberry Pi OS (Legacy) and repeat the manufacturer's script. The serial line comes from the Raspberry Pi firmware overlay list; after restarting, the port name appears with ls /dev/ttyAMA*.

Power and wiring

Where each wire goes

Two 18650 batteries in series provide 6 to 8.4 V. From there, two separate lines supply 5.1 V to the Raspberry Pi and Arduino, and 6 V to the servos.

⚠️ Lithium battery safety

  • None of this has been bench-tested. The wiring diagram is based on the component datasheets. Check each connection with a multimeter before turning on the switch.
  • Polarity: red to positive, black to negative. Measure before each new connection. Reversed wires burn out the board immediately.
  • Adjust before connecting the load: set the LM2596 to 5.1 V and the UBEC to 6 V, measured with a multimeter, with the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, and servos disconnected.
  • A short circuit can start a fire: solder with the cells OUT of the holder and insulate each splice with heat-shrink tubing. The 3 A fuse goes right after the holder.
  • Matching cells: use two new 18650 cells from the same brand and model. Take dented, swollen, or hot cells to a battery collection point, not the robot.
  • First charge: stay nearby, with the robot on a surface that won’t burn.
2 × 18650 in series (2S pack)→ fuse + protection board + switch→ LM2596: 5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi and Arduino · UBEC: 6 V for the 2 servos

5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi and Arduino

The LM2596 converter keeps the 5 V output steady until the battery drops to about 6.5 V. Its output goes to a terminal block in the base. From there, one pair of 20 AWG wires runs up the neck to the Pi's USB-C adapter, and another pair goes to the + and − pins of the Sensor Shield's COM connector. The board can handle about 2 A continuously without a heatsink (typical value, NC): use the Pi 4, or the Pi 5 without USB accessories. If the Pi reports undervoltage (the vcgencmd get_throttled command returns anything other than throttled=0x0), shorten this pair of wires and check the 5.1 V.

6 V for the servos

The UBEC supplies 3 A continuously to both MG996R servos through the Sensor Shield's blue screw terminal, with the SEL jumper removed. If the jumper were in place, the 6 V would flow into the Arduino's 5 V line. A 1000 µF capacitor at the terminal helps handle the startup surge.

Charging via USB-C

The balance charger sits with its port at a window behind the base and accepts a USB-C power supply up to 15 W. It needs a third wire, soldered to the point joining the two cells. It connects before the switch, so the robot charges while switched off.

The Raspberry Pi and Arduino communicate over serial

The Pi sits in the head and the Arduino in the base, and a USB plug won’t fit through the 9 mm neck hole. That’s why the connection uses a 3-wire serial link (UART): the Pi sends through pin 32 and listens on pin 33. These numbers are the pin positions in the 40-pin row, counted from the microSD card corner; they are not GPIO numbers. The Arduino runs at 5 V and the Pi at 3.3 V: 3 1 kΩ resistors, secured to the terminal block, lower the voltage on the wire from the Arduino to the Pi. On the Pi system, serial is enabled with a line in the configuration file: dtoverlay=uart5 on the Pi 4 or dtoverlay=uart4-pi5 on the Pi 5, always after the display script (prep step 4).

The KCD11-101 switch is rated for 3 A at 250 V~ and 6 A at 125 V~ (store listing), with no rating for direct current. That is why the fuse is 3 A, the switch's lowest rating, and slow-blow (marked T3A): servo startup and capacitor charging cause peaks lasting a fraction of a second that would blow a fast fuse without a fault. This keeps normal use below the rating, lets a seconds-long peak pass without blowing the fuse, and makes a sustained overload blow the fuse before the switch heats up (typical behavior for a glass fuse, NC). The phase 2 program must not let both servos push against their limits at the same time.

  • Each STL is a closed mesh and fits on the 256 mm build plate.
  • No part passes through another part or component in 10 head positions.
  • Each part goes in and comes out through the side indicated in the assembly instructions.

  • No part needs supports in the file’s print position.

⚠️ What only printing can tell you

  • The actual clearances on your printer: that’s why the test part comes first.
  • The dimensions marked NC on the purchased components.
  • The actual time and weight: the numbers are estimates without a slicer.
  • The strength of the gears and fork in use.
  • The electrical section: the wiring diagram was based on the component datasheets and has not been bench-tested.
