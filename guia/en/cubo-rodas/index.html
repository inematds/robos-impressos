In short

The square robot on wheels is 31 cm tall and is designed for you to print at home and assemble. The head is a cube with a 5-inch screen for a face; it turns almost a full rotation and tilts up and down. Below, two motors with encoders drive two 85 mm wheels, and a loose ball supports the rear. Inside are a Raspberry Pi, an Arduino, two servos, and three 18650 batteries that charge via USB-C. You need a 3D printer with a 256 mm build plate, the parts on the shopping list, and a soldering iron. This page covers phase 1: the body assembled, the wires connected, and both servos centered. Programming (movement, screen face, voice, camera, AI) is phase 2 and has not been done yet.

The robot

What you’ll assemble

A cube with a face, a slim neck, and a body on two wheels. No screws are visible from the front: everything is fastened underneath, at the back, or behind the wheels.

Inside

The screen, Raspberry Pi, and tilt servo go in the head. On the deck, the middle tray, go the Arduino, motor driver, power converters, and the servo that turns the neck. The 2 motors and battery go in the base; the battery comes out through a hatch underneath. The cables run up through the hollow neck.

What supports the weight

The load-bearing parts are made of PETG: base, battery hatch, motor flanges, deck, torso, neck, gears, fork, and head frame. The white head shell and dark face panel are made of PLA and simply fit over the head.

↔️ Rotates ±170°

A standard 180° servo moves two printed gears with 44 and 22 teeth. The neck rotates twice as far as the servo: almost a full turn.

↕️ Tilts from −25° to +30°

The tilt servo is hidden inside the head. From the outside, you only see the slim neck.

🙂 Moves on two wheels

Two 12 V motors with encoders drive the 85 mm front wheels. At the back, a 40 mm ball rolls freely. The calculated top speed is 0.48 m/s, and it can get over steps up to about 8 mm high.

The project checker verifies the balance with the head in 11 positions, and the model calculates how far the robot can brake and accelerate without tipping over. The results are at the end of this page and in the robot plan.

⚠️ Risks to consider before buying

What hasn't been tested yet. Read before spending money.

  • Nothing has been printed or assembled yet. The electrical section of this page has not been bench-tested: it was based on the component datasheets.
  • The 3MF files for the build plates have never been opened in Bambu Studio. If one won’t open, use the STL files.
  • The 3 A switch is the weakest point in the electrical system. Based on the component specs, the robot draws about 2 A during normal use and about 6.5 A in the worst case (both wheels stalled, both servos pushing, and the Raspberry Pi at full load). The 3 A fuse protects the switch, but it may blow if the robot pushes against an obstacle. Replacing it with a larger switch means changing the 13.5 × 9 mm cutout in the base.
  • The screen’s 2 cables, HDMI and USB, are not included in the 3D model. The model leaves about 22 mm below the Raspberry Pi and about 30 mm beside its USB ports: the straight cables that come with the screen will not fit. This is the main fit risk for this robot. Check with the parts in hand before closing the head.
  • The charger comes set up for 2 cells. Converting it to 3 requires removing a tiny resistor and closing a solder jumper. If that doesn’t work, the 3 cells can be removed through the battery hatch and charged in a standard 18650 charger.
  • The labels printed on the solder pads of the 3-cell protection board and on the charger’s 2 middle solder pads could not be read. Check each cell junction with a multimeter (about 4 V and about 8 V) before soldering.
  • The position of the motor’s 6 holes around the shaft is an unconfirmed measurement. If they don’t line up, the part to reprint is the flange, a small disk. Check with the motor in hand before printing the base.
  • The Raspberry Pi gets 5.1 V through pins 2 and 4, bypassing the USB-C input protection. The wrong voltage or a reversed wire there will burn out the Pi. Measure at the terminal block before connecting it.
  • The test coupon does not check the MG996R servo window or the M5 screw thread. Check the servo window on the head frame (plate 01). The M5 thread is on the fork arm (plate 04). Check both before starting the long prints.
  • To reprogram the Arduino after assembling the robot, loosen the 4 bottom screws and lift the torso with the neck and head.
  • The fuse holder, terminal block, and 3 serial resistors have no modeled location: they stay loose and insulated with heat-shrink tubing. Each motor cable is about 14 cm long and may not reach the motor driver; if it falls short, splice it.
  • The Sensor Shield’s A0 and A1 connectors, used by the second wire from each encoder, are not clearly visible in the store photo. If your board doesn’t have these pins, leave the 2 green wires insulated: the phase 2 program can count rotations using only the yellow wire.
  • Measurements marked NC come from store listings. Measure your part before printing the section that touches it.
  • The path for the 8 wires through the fork hole and the slot in the bottom of the head shell has never been tested with printed parts.
  • Balance: the checker shows that the robot does not tip over when stationary, with the head in any position. While moving, the calculation is an estimate: it can brake at up to about 3 m/s² without tipping forward, and the phase 2 program should use no more than half that. The highest step it can climb is about 8 mm.
  • Heat: the Raspberry Pi 4 is enclosed in the head, which is made of PLA and has no ventilation opening. PLA softens near 60 °C, and the Pi throttles starting at 80 °C. Attach the heatsinks and measure with the vcgencmd measure_temp command. If it goes above 70 °C, print the head shell in PETG or open ventilation holes at the back.

The process

From file to robot in 4 steps

The order matters: the first print takes less than an hour and helps you avoid losing a whole day of printer time.

1
Print the test pieceA small test coupon with threaded insert holes in three sizes and screw holes. You can find out which size works with your printer before using up filament.
2
Print the 8 build platesThe files already have the parts laid flat and sorted by color. It takes about 43 hours and 1143 g of filament, with no supports on any part.
3
Prepare the power, cables, and servos on the workbenchBefore closing any parts, you solder the power wires and motor cables, adjust both voltages with the multimeter, and center the two servos with a program ready for the Arduino.
4
Set the threaded inserts and assemble31 brass threaded inserts with a soldering iron, then the base with the motors, the deck with the boards, the head on the workbench, and finally the torso with the head on top. The robot charges via USB-C without opening it.

Glossary

Words used on this page

Electronics and 3D printing terms used in this guide, explained in one or two sentences each.

GPIO
The Raspberry Pi’s 40-pin row, where the power and signal wires connect. In this guide, "pin 32" is the pin’s position in the row; "GPIO12" is the name of the signal that comes out through it.
UBEC
A small converter used in model aircraft that lowers the battery voltage to a fixed value. Here, 6 V for the servos.
BMS
Lithium battery protection board. It disconnects the battery before it discharges too much, overcharges, or shorts out.
3S
Three lithium cells connected in series, one after another. Their voltages add up: from 9 V (empty) to 12.6 V (full).
Cell junction (battery pack)
The point where one cell connects to the next. A 3-cell pack has 2 junctions. The protection board and charger measure each cell through them.
Horn
The plastic part that fits onto the servo shaft and turns with it. This robot uses the round, disk-shaped horn.
Heat-set threaded insert
A brass bushing with an internal thread. It goes into the hole in the plastic when pushed in with a soldering iron, creating a metal thread.
NC
Unconfirmed: a measurement from a store listing that has not yet been checked against the part in hand.
Slicer
The software that turns the 3D file into the layers the printer prints. Bambu Lab's slicer is called Bambu Studio.
SSH
A way to use the Raspberry Pi over the network: you type commands in your computer's terminal, and they run on the Pi without connecting a keyboard or monitor to it.
UART (serial)
A simple data connection: one wire to send (TX) and one to receive (RX). The Raspberry Pi sends commands to the Arduino through it.
Motor driver (H-bridge)
Board that connects the motors to the battery and reverses their direction when the Arduino sends a command. Here, it’s the L298N.
Encoder
Sensor attached to the back of the motor that counts rotations. The program uses it to know how far each wheel has moved.
SEL jumper
A small plastic cap over 2 Sensor Shield pins, next to the blue screw terminal, marked SEL. When installed, it connects the Arduino's 5 V to the servo connectors. When removed, the servos receive only the power supplied through the blue screw terminal.
Screw terminal
A screw connector: the wire goes into the hole and the screw tightens it. On the Sensor Shield, it is the blue block marked GND and VCC.
Sketch
The name Arduino gives to a program. You open the file in Arduino IDE and upload it to the board with the USB cable.
Shield
Board that plugs into the Arduino’s pins and adds connectors. Here, the Sensor Shield provides connectors for the servos, motor driver, and encoders.
AWG
A measure of wire thickness. The lower the number, the thicker the wire: 20 AWG is thicker than 22 AWG.
GND
The circuit’s negative, also called ground. All the robot’s negative connections are connected to each other.
Dupont
A thin wire that comes with a plug-in connector on each end, sized to fit the pins on the boards. Also called a jumper.
Step-down
Converter that lowers the voltage. Here, the XL4015 lowers the battery voltage to 5.1 V.
Heat-shrink tubing

Printing

How to print

Bambu Lab A1, 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer. PETG for load-bearing parts, PLA for parts that only cover something.

Suggestion for the A1, not yet tested with these parts: use the textured PEI plate for both materials. In Bambu Studio, choose the Generic PLA profile for PLA and Generic PETG for PETG, and keep the temperatures set by the profile. Typical values to check: PLA with the nozzle at 220 °C and the build plate at 55–65 °C; PETG with the nozzle at 250–255 °C and the build plate at 70–80 °C. Bambu’s filament guide lists 240 to 270 °C for the PETG nozzle (source).

Assembly

How to assemble

First, four workbench prep steps with the parts still loose: all the soldering happens here. Then assembly, in the same order the checker tested: each part goes in from the indicated side without hitting anything already assembled.

Before assembly: 4 workbench prep steps

These come first because, once the robot is closed, you can no longer reach the converter adjustment, the Arduino USB port, or the Raspberry Pi pins and card. All soldering on the robot is done here; assembly only uses screw terminals and fittings. Prep steps 1, 2, and 3 are electrical and have not been bench-tested: measure each connection with the multimeter before turning on the switch. The sequence is the same as in the wiring file, step by step.

1

Solder the power wires and adjust both voltages

You assemble the wiring harness by following the wiring diagram, with the printed base and deck side by side. Don’t connect the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, or servos yet.

🔰 Never soldered before? Read this before turning on the iron

  • Practice first. Make about ten wire splices using scrap wire before touching the robot's parts. Wear safety glasses and solder in a well-ventilated area: the iron tip gets hotter than 300 °C.
  • Tin both parts. Melt a little solder onto the stripped wire and a little onto the terminal, separately. Then touch them together and heat them: they join in 2 or 3 seconds.
  • Heat the joint, not the solder. Touch the iron to the wire and terminal at the same time, and feed the solder to the hot joint, not to the iron tip.
  • A good solder joint is smooth and wraps around the wire. Dull, grainy, or ball-shaped solder is a cold joint: it makes poor contact. Heat it again until it flows.
  • Check with a multimeter. Set it to continuity mode, the one that beeps, and test each joint. Before putting the cells in the holder, check that the positive and negative terminals are not connected.
  • Never solder directly to an 18650 cell. Heat damages the cell, and it can leak or catch fire. On this robot, solder the battery connection to the holder's metal strip, with the cells out of the holder. Another option is to ask a battery shop for a pre-soldered pack with wires: in that case, first check that it fits where the holder goes.
  • Get help with the battery. If you are under 18 or don't feel safe, ask an adult or an electronics technician to do the battery soldering.

Tutorial in Portuguese, with photos: How to solder: learn to make a perfect solder joint (Fazedores blog, by Manoel Lemos).

  1. With the 3 cells out of the holder, solder 2 yellow 20 cm wires to each of the 2 metal strips joining the cells: these are the pack’s 2 junctions, and each one has one wire going to the protection board and another to the charger. Mark the 2 wires from the junction on the red-wire side of the holder with tape: this is the upper junction.
  2. Set up the charger for 3 cells, following the picture on the store page: remove the tiny resistor shown and bridge the jumper next to the ROV label with a small drop of solder. Without this, it only charges up to 8.4 V. If you’re not used to working with such small solder joints, don’t use this charger: remove the 3 cells through the battery hatch and charge them in a regular 18650 charger.
  3. Solder 2 red 20 AWG wires, 15 cm long, to the switch’s 2 terminals. Feed the free ends through the cutout at the back of the base, from outside in, and fit the switch into the cutout. Do this BEFORE soldering the switch to the rest: once connected to the wiring harness, it won’t fit through the 13.5 × 9 mm cutout.
  4. With the base beside you as a guide for wire length, connect the holder, fuse, protection board, and charger according to the wiring table. Leave about 15 cm of slack in the holder’s 4 wires: it moves down with the battery hatch when you replace the cells.
  5. Solder one end of the switch to the protection board’s positive output (P+). Strip 6 mm from the other end: it goes to terminal 4. Solder a 20 cm black 20 AWG wire to the negative output (P−) and strip its free end: it goes to terminal 5.
  6. Now prepare the deck, still outside the robot. Cut a piece with 5 terminals from the terminal block and number them 1 to 5. Solder 2 short 20 AWG wires to the XL4015 input and tighten the other ends in the terminal block: IN+ to terminal 4, IN− to terminal 5. Connect the UBEC’s red input wire to terminal 4 and its black wire to terminal 5.
  7. Remove the 3 jumpers (the plastic caps) from the L298N motor driver: the 2 marked ENA and ENB and the 5 V regulator jumper next to the power screw terminal. Then, on the same side of the terminal block, tighten a red 20 AWG wire from terminal 4 to the driver's +12V terminal and a black wire from terminal 5 to the driver's GND terminal.
  8. At the XL4015 output, solder 2 short 20 AWG wires and tighten the other ends in the terminal block: OUT+ to terminal 1, OUT− to terminal 2. Do not connect anything else to the outputs.
  9. Place the base and deck side by side. On the other side of the terminal block, tighten the red wire from the switch into terminal 4 and the black wire from the protection board into terminal 5.
  10. Put the 3 cells in the holder, checking which way the positive ends face. With the multimeter, measure from the holder’s black wire to each yellow wire: about 4 V at the lower junction and about 8 V at the upper junction (the one marked with tape). Turn on the switch.
  11. Measure between terminals 4 and 5: it should read 9 to 12.6 V, with red on positive.
  12. With the multimeter across terminals 1 and 2, turn the XL4015 adjustment screw with the screwdriver until it reads 5.1 V. It takes several turns.
  13. Measure the UBEC output: it should read 6 V. If it reads 5 V, move its jumper to 6 V.
  14. Turn off the switch.
2

Upload the program to the Arduino and set both servos to 90°

The program is the sketch centrar_servos.ino. It moves both servos to the middle point and keeps them there.

  1. Open the centrar_servos.ino sketch in Arduino IDE. Connect the Arduino Uno to the computer with the USB cable, without the shield or battery.
  2. From the Tools menu, select the Arduino Uno board and the port it appeared on. Click Upload.
  3. Unplug the USB cable, fit the Sensor Shield onto the Uno, and remove the shield’s SEL jumper: it is the small plastic cap over 2 pins, next to the blue screw terminal.
  4. Reconnect the USB cable just to take a measurement with the multimeter. The shield's COM connector has 4 upright pins labeled TX, RX, −, and +. You should get about 5 V between + and −. On the D9 connector, you should get 0 V between the V pin and the G pin.
  5. If the D9 V pin reads 5 V, the SEL jumper is still in place. If the COM + pin does not read 5 V, your shield differs from the store photo: look for another pin labeled + or 5V that reads 5 V with SEL removed. A wrong pin here sends the servos' 6 V to the Raspberry Pi.
  6. Unplug the USB cable.
  7. The UBEC output has a 3-pin servo plug: cut it off and strip the wires. Red is positive and black (or brown) is negative. If there’s a third wire, white or orange, insulate its end with heat-shrink tubing. Clamp the red wire into VCC and the black wire into GND on the blue screw terminal. The 1000 µF capacitor goes into the same terminal, with its longer lead in VCC.
  8. Take 2 40 cm jumper wires, cut the connector off one end of each, and strip the wires. Clamp one in terminal 1 of the terminal block, opposite the converter, and plug its connector into the + pin on the COM connector. Connect the other from terminal 2 to the − pin on the same connector. Leave the TX and RX pins beside it empty: 5 V on one of them reaches the Raspberry Pi through the serial wire.
  9. Connect the pan servo to D9 and the tilt servo to D10: brown or black wire to G, red to V, orange or yellow to S.
  10. Turn on the switch: both servos move to the center (90°) and the Uno LED blinks once per second. The motor driver is not connected to the Arduino yet: the wheels will not move.
  11. Turn off the switch. From now on, do not turn the servo shafts by hand.
  12. Disconnect the test setup for assembly: remove the 2 servos from D9 and D10, disconnect the UBEC wires and capacitor from the blue terminal, and remove the 2 jumpers from the COM connector. Disconnect the 2 wires from the base from terminals 4 and 5: you will reconnect them during assembly, after passing them through the slot in the deck. If the shield covers the Uno mounting holes, remove it too. Take the 3 cells out of the holder: they go back in during assembly, in the battery step.

Don’t power the MG996R servo from USB: it supplies 0.5 A, and the servo needs more when starting. Never connect the computer’s USB cable while the battery is connected. Upload the sketch before closing the robot: afterward, you need to lift the torso to reach the Arduino USB port.

3

Make the cables: serial, Raspberry Pi power, driver, and encoders

The Raspberry Pi goes in the head and the Arduino on the deck. They communicate over 3 wires that run down through the neck. Here you prepare those wires, the 3-resistor divider that protects the Pi from the Arduino’s 5 V, the cable that powers the Pi, the driver’s 6 wires, and the cables for the 2 motors. There’s no more soldering after this prep step.

  1. Serial. Set aside 3 40 cm jumpers: one green, one blue, and one black (or mark 3 jumpers of another color with tape). Leave the green and black ones intact, with both female connectors. On the blue one, cut off ONE connector and strip 6 mm: this end goes into a terminal.
  2. Find the pins on the Raspberry Pi: pin 1 is at the corner beside the microSD card, in the inner row; pin 40 is at the opposite corner, in the outer row. Odd-numbered pins are in the inner row and even-numbered pins are in the outer row. Pins 2, 4, and 6 are the first 3 in the outer row; pin 9 is the 5th in the inner row. Pins 32 and 34 are the 16th and 17th in the outer row; pin 33 is the 17th in the inner row.
  3. Mark the end of each serial jumper that goes to the Pi with tape: green = pin 32, blue = pin 33, black = pin 34.
  4. Divider. Solder 2 1 kΩ resistors together, leg to leg, and cover the splice with heat-shrink tubing. This pair is 2 kΩ.
  5. Cut a jumper in half. Solder the cut end of one half to a leg of the 3rd resistor and cover it with heat-shrink tubing. The female connector on this half goes to D1.
  6. On the terminal block, on the converter side, tighten the free leg of the 3rd resistor together with one end of the 2 kΩ pair in terminal 3. Tighten the other end of the pair in terminal 2, together with the black wire already there. Bend the end of each leg before tightening, so the screw grips it.
  7. Check with the multimeter in ohms: between the D1 jumper’s female connector and terminal 3, about 1 kΩ; between terminal 3 and terminal 2, about 2 kΩ.
  8. Raspberry Pi power. Cut 2 jumpers in half: you’ll have 4 halves with female connectors. Cut 45 cm of red wire and 45 cm of black 20 AWG wire. Solder 2 halves to the end of the red wire and 2 to the end of the black wire, and cover each splice with heat-shrink tubing. Mark the 2 red ones with tape: they go to pins 2 and 4 (5 V). The 2 black ones go to pins 6 and 9 (GND). Strip 6 mm from the other end of each wire: they go to terminals 1 and 2.
  9. Motor driver. Its 3 jumpers were already removed in prep step 1. Where the ENA and ENB jumpers were, there are 2 pins in each spot: one is the input (ENA or ENB) and the other is connected to the board's 5 V. With the multimeter set to continuity and the switch off, find the pin in each pair that beeps with the driver's +5V terminal: leave that one empty. Mark the other with a pen: that's where the Arduino wire goes. A wire from the Arduino to a 5 V pin can burn out the Arduino output.
  10. Set aside 6 intact jumpers for the motor driver signals and label each one with tape: ENA, IN1, IN2, IN3, IN4, and ENB.
  11. Tighten a 15 cm red 22 AWG wire into terminal 1 of the terminal block, on the side opposite the converter. The other end goes to the driver’s +5V terminal during assembly, along with the encoders’ blue wires.
  12. Encoders. Each motor comes with a 6-wire cable. Plug the cable into the motor and cut off the connector at the other end. Strip 6 mm from the red, white, blue, and black wires: they go into terminals. The middle pin (V) always stays empty: with the SEL jumper removed, it carries the servos’ 6 V, and 6 V on a signal wire will burn out the Raspberry Pi or encoder.

Where pin 1 is and why the pin number isn’t the GPIO number: Raspberry Pi documentation. The same page says the 5 V pins accept power input for the Pi. With the Pi powered on, the pinout command draws the pin map. About 20 mm remain between the Pi and screen (unconfirmed measurement): a standard 14 mm Dupont connector fits upright.

Flash the microSD card, test the screen, and connect the serial

The card is flashed on your computer with a microSD card reader. Then you connect to the Raspberry Pi over the network using SSH: you type commands on your computer, and they run on the Pi.

  1. Install Raspberry Pi Imager, the free Raspberry Pi program, on your computer, and put the card in the reader.
  2. In Imager, choose your Pi model, the Raspberry Pi OS (64-bit) operating system, and the card.
  3. Before flashing, open Imager’s settings: give the Pi a name (for example, robo), create a username and password, enter your Wi-Fi name and password, and enable SSH. Flash the card.
  4. Put the card in the Pi. For this test only, connect the screen to it with the HDMI cable and USB cable, and power the Pi with a 5 V, 3 A USB-C supply. Wait about 2 minutes: the screen should show the system. The manufacturer says it works with Raspberry Pi OS without installing a driver.
  5. On your computer, open the terminal: on Windows, use the PowerShell program; on Mac and Linux, use Terminal.
  6. Type ssh usuario@robo.local, replacing usuario and robo with the names you created. Answer yes the first time and enter the password.
  7. If robo.local doesn’t respond, open your router’s page, find the Pi in the list of connected devices, and use its number (the IP address), for example ssh usuario@192.168.0.50.
  8. When the terminal shows the Pi’s name, you’re in. Connect the serial: type sudo nano /boot/firmware/config.txt, go to the end of the file, and add a line: dtoverlay=uart5 on Raspberry Pi 4, or dtoverlay=uart4-pi5 on Raspberry Pi 5. Save with Ctrl+O and Enter, then exit with Ctrl+X.
  9. Type sudo poweroff, wait for the Pi’s green LED to stop blinking, unplug the power supply, and disconnect the 2 screen cables: you’ll reconnect them during assembly.

The screen is the Waveshare 5inch HDMI LCD (B), with 800 × 480 resolution, connected via HDMI. The manufacturer says it works on Raspberry Pi OS without a driver (manufacturer’s page); if the image doesn’t fill the screen, see its wiki. Not bench-tested. Raspberry Pi documentation on GitHub: SSH and how to find the Pi’s address. The serial line comes from the Raspberry Pi firmware overlay list; after restarting, the port name appears with ls /dev/ttyAMA*.

Power and wiring

Where each wire goes

Three 18650 batteries in series provide 9 to 12.6 V. From there, three lines run: 5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, driver, and encoders; 6 V for the servos; and pack voltage, through the driver, for the motors.

⚠️ Lithium battery safety

  • None of this has been bench-tested. The wiring diagram was based on the component datasheets. Check each connection with the multimeter before turning on the switch.
  • Polarity: red to positive, black to negative. Measure before each new connection. Reversed wires can burn out the board immediately.
  • Adjust before connecting the load: XL4015 to 5.1 V and UBEC to 6 V, measured with the multimeter, with the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, and servos disconnected.
  • A short circuit can start a fire: solder with the cells OUT of the holder and insulate each splice with heat-shrink tubing. The 3 A fuse goes right after the holder.
  • Matching cells: all three 18650 batteries must be the same brand and model, and new. A dented, swollen, or hot cell goes to battery recycling, not in the robot.
  • First charge: stay nearby, with the robot on a nonflammable surface.
3 × 18650 in series (3S pack)→ fuse + protection board + switch→ XL4015: 5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, and driver · UBEC: 6 V for the 2 servos · L298N: the 2 motors

5.1 V for the Raspberry Pi, Arduino, driver, and encoders

The XL4015 converter reduces the pack voltage to 5.1 V. Its output goes to a terminal block on the deck. From there, one pair of 20 AWG wires runs up through the neck to Pi pins 2, 4, 6, and 9; another pair goes to the + and − pins of the Sensor Shield COM connector; and one wire goes to the driver’s +5V screw terminal, which also supplies 5 V to the 2 encoders. If the Pi reports undervoltage (the vcgencmd get_throttled command returns anything other than throttled=0x0), shorten the wire pair and check the 5.1 V.

6 V for the servos, pack voltage for the motors

The UBEC supplies a continuous 3 A to the two MG996R servos through the Sensor Shield’s blue screw terminal, with the SEL jumper removed. With the jumper in place, 6 V would reach the Arduino’s 5 V line. A 1000 µF capacitor at the terminal handles the startup peak. The 2 motors receive pack voltage through the L298N motor driver, which drops about 2 V along the way. The Arduino controls the driver with 6 wires.

Charging via USB-C

The balance charger sits with its port in a window behind the base and accepts a USB-C power supply of up to 15 W. It comes ready for 2 cells; for 3, you need to remove a tiny resistor and close a solder jumper. It uses 2 extra wires, soldered to the cell junctions. It connects before the switch, so the robot charges while switched off.

The Raspberry Pi and Arduino communicate over serial

The Pi sits in the head and the Arduino on the deck, and a USB plug won’t fit through the neck’s 9 mm hole. So they connect through a 3-wire serial link (UART): the Pi transmits through pin 32 and receives through pin 33. These numbers indicate the pin’s position in the 40-pin row, counted from the microSD card corner; they are not GPIO numbers. The Arduino runs at 5 V and the Pi at 3.3 V: 3 1 kΩ resistors, secured to the terminal block, lower the voltage on the wire from the Arduino to the Pi. On the Pi, enable serial with one line in the configuration file: dtoverlay=uart5 on Pi 4 or dtoverlay=uart4-pi5 on Pi 5 (prep step 4).

The project started from a reference drawing for each robot. The measurement compares only the outline: the model's silhouette is placed over the drawing's silhouette from the same view, and the number (IoU) is the shared area divided by the total area of both. It ranges from 0 to 1; the higher it is, the closer the outlines match. Color, eyes, and details are not included.

  • Balance while braking and accelerating: the calculation is an estimate.
