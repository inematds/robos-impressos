Robots to print at home · phase 1: the body · project not yet printed

Your pet robot starts with your 3D printer

You want a creature-like robot at home. Ready-made kits are expensive, and loose projects from the internet often don’t fit: hours of printing and filament wasted. robos-impressos provides the body: three models with a place for each motor and board, checked by a program before you print (you confirm the fit on your printer with a small test piece). Making it move on its own, with wiring and programming, comes in the next phase.

See the 3 robots View on GitHub
At a glance

You want to build a friendly robot at home and don’t know where to start with the body. robos-impressos is a set of three robot bodies to 3D print: a dinosaur and a capybara about 33 cm tall that roll across the floor, and a square desktop robot with a screen for a face. Each one already has the right space for the motors, servos (small motors that turn to an angle and stop), battery, and Arduino and Raspberry Pi boards. It’s for anyone with access to a 3D printer with a 256 mm build plate who wants to build, learn, or teach robotics. This is phase 1, just the body and its fittings: wiring, voice, and intelligence are for phase 2. And an honest note: this is a digital project checked by a script; no one has printed these parts yet.

The two banners above are AI-generated illustrations. All other images on this page are renders: computer-generated images made from the project files, not photos of an assembled robot.

The 3 robots

Choose yours

All three use the same servos and screws. The dinosaur and capybara share the same skeleton inside: only the shell changes.

🦖 Dinosaur

A big-eyed baby dinosaur with blue spikes. It rolls on two wheels hidden inside its legs and rests on a ball. It turns and tilts its head, opens its mouth, and moves both arms.

5 servos + 2 motors

🦫 Capybara

Seated, with a green bandana. The wheels sit inside the thighs, behind the blue ring, and the support ball sits inside the tail. It turns and tilts its head and opens its mouth.

🤖 Square robot

Made for a desk; it doesn’t roll. Its head is a cube with a 3.5-inch screen that displays its eyes and mouth. It turns almost halfway around in either direction (nearly a full turn in total) and tilts up and down.

How they move

Inside: a skeleton with the shell on top

The dark skeleton bears the weight. The colorful shell only covers it: it comes off in sections without taking the skeleton apart.

The skeleton

One piece forms the floor, the walls where the motors are screwed in, and the neck support. The Arduino tray and Raspberry Pi tray slide between the walls like drawers. The battery sits underneath them.

The shell in parts

The belly, back, legs, tail, and head are separate parts, held together by hidden screws. To reach the boards, remove the head, tail, and back (7 screws), then slide the trays out like drawers.

Neck without a neck

The top of the body is a sphere, and the head is a helmet that turns and tilts around it with 2 mm of clearance (3 mm for the capybara). That lets the head move in every direction without leaving a gap.

The square one turns almost a full rotation

A standard servo only turns halfway. Two printed gears double that rotation, and the cables run up inside the hollow neck tube without getting tangled.

✅ What the script guarantees

  • Each file is a closed solid made of a single body.
  • Every part fits on the 256 × 256 × 256 mm build plate.
  • No part occupies the space of another part or a component (servo, motor, board, battery), with the head, mouth, and arms at the extremes.
  • Every part can reach its position: the script brings them in one at a time, in assembly order, and flags any collision with what is already assembled.
  • No part is floating in the air, and the center of gravity falls inside the supports, with clearance.
  • The test itself is tested: one part is deliberately moved 3 mm, and the script must flag it.

⚠️ What only printing can tell you

  • No one has printed or assembled these parts yet.
  • Print time and filament use are volume-based estimates.
  • Some component dimensions have no official source (servo tabs, motor driver holes). They are marked NC in cad/parametros.py and section 4 of PLANO.md (in Portuguese): measure your part with calipers and, if it differs, change the number and generate the parts again.
  • The script does not test strength, whether a screwdriver can reach every screw, or the 3 mm fit of the servos in the arms and mouth.
Action plan

From file to assembled robot body

Five steps, no programming needed. At the end, you have the assembled body, with servos and motors in place; making it move is phase 2.

1
Choose a modelThe square robot has fewer parts (14), but needs a Raspberry Pi and screen. The dinosaur and capybara each have about 38 parts, and you can swap one shell for the other later.
2
Buy the minimum for testingOne pack of M3 threaded inserts, some M3 screws, and a soldering iron are enough for the test; for the animals, you also need a micro servo (the square robot does not use one). The rest (servos, wheel motors, battery holder, switch) are common parts at electronics shops in Brazil. The servos and motors are only for checking the fit at this stage: they will not move yet.
3
Print the test part firstIt’s a 7 cm test plate that checks the fit of the micro servo, inserts, screws, and guide pin on your printer. Less than an hour of printing can save you from redoing a five-hour part. If everything fits, you can continue without installing anything.
4
Print by colorThe parts come laid out in the right orientation and separated by color. The skeleton is printed in PETG, which is more durable; the shell in PLA.
5
Assemble from the inside outFirst the skeleton with motors and servos, then the shell, and finally the head. The order, screw by screw, is in the project plan.

Files, commands, and the shopping list are in the technical section: see the technical guide ↓

Words on this page

If any of these names are new to you

Servo
A small motor that turns to an angle and stops there. It turns the head and opens the mouth.
Arduino and Raspberry Pi
Two electronic boards. Arduino controls motors and servos; Raspberry Pi is a small computer that handles voice and camera in phase 2.
Script
A small program. Here, it generates the parts and checks that they’re correct.
Render
An image made on a computer from the 3D file. It isn’t a photo.
PLA and PETG
Two printing plastics. PLA gives a nice finish; PETG handles more force and heat.
Build plate
The printer’s base. “12 build plates” means 12 prints, one after another.
M3 insert
A threaded brass sleeve that goes into the plastic while hot so the 3 mm screw holds firmly.
18650 battery
A rechargeable lithium battery, a little larger than a regular battery.
Motor driver
A small board that lets Arduino start and reverse the wheel motors.
TT motor
The small yellow geared motor commonly used in robotics cars.
Shield
A board that fits on top of Arduino to make connecting the servos easier.
Rocker switch
The on/off switch.
Chassis, fork and cradle
Skeleton parts: the chassis is the base with the walls; the fork and cradle are the two neck parts.
Pan, tilt, horn and CG
Terms in the checker output: pan means turning side to side; tilt means angling up and down; horn is the small part attached to the servo shaft; CG is the center of gravity.
Guide pin
A printed 4 mm pin that aligns the front and back halves of the torso. The hole it goes into is the socket.
NC
Not confirmed: a component measurement without an official source that you check on your own part.
Technical section

The kit: for anyone who wants to print, change dimensions, or create another model

You don't need this section to understand what you've read above.

What the kit is

Parts generated from code, not drawn by hand

Everything comes from a file of measurements. If you change the servo or your printer’s clearance, change one number and generate it again.

📐 A file of measurements

cad/parametros.py stores clearances, measurements for each component (with the source next to it), and the shape of each robot.

🔍 Checker with 9 tests

cad/verificar.py reads the exported STL files and rejects open parts, oversized parts, thin walls, parts intersecting each other, parts that cannot reach their position, and robots that tip over.

🦴 Shared skeleton

The chassis, trays, fork, and neck cradle use the same files for the dinosaur and the capybara (pecas/comum/).

How it works

From measurements to print files

The animals’ outer shells are made by adding rounded shapes together and turning them into a mesh; holes, pockets, and cuts are exact operations applied on top.

cad/parametros.py→ cad/gerar.py→ pecas/<modelo>/stl + 3mf + montagem.json→ cad/verificar.py→ cad/renders.sh

Generate

Creates STL and 3MF files for each part, already oriented for printing, the complete assembly (montagem.glb), and the build plate layouts.

Check

Assembles the robot from the files, moves the joints to their limits, measures overlaps by shared volume, and rebuilds the assembly one part at a time.

Render

Blender produces the images on this page: assembled, exploded, skeleton, and build plates.

Prerequisites

What you need

To just print, all you need is a printer, a slicer, and the ZIP download. Python and Blender are for anyone who wants to change the project.

For printing

A printer with a 256 mm build plate (the project was designed for the Bambu Lab A1; it does not fit on a 220 mm plate because the largest part is 226 mm), PLA and PETG filament, a slicer such as Bambu Studio, and the components on the shopping list.

To generate the parts

Python 3.12 or later (download it from python.org; the library versions require it) and git. The libraries are installed in an isolated environment inside the folder.

# check if you already have it
python3 --version
git --version

For the images (optional)

Blender 4. It is only used for the renders.

# check if you already have it
blender --version
User guide · step by step

Download, print, and assemble

These are the repository commands. The complete assembly sequence, with every screw, is in PLANO.md (in Portuguese).

1

Download the project

The print files are ready in the pecas/ folder, in STL and 3MF formats. If you only want to print, download the ZIP (about 80 MB), extract it, and open the files in your slicer: you do not need a terminal or Python. The componentes/ folders are only outlines of purchased parts: do not print them.

Download everything as a ZIP

If you want to change dimensions and regenerate the parts, you can clone the repository with git in a terminal:

git clone https://github.com/inematds/robos-impressos.git
cd robos-impressos
ls pecas/dino/stl pecas/capivara/stl pecas/cubo/stl pecas/comum/stl
2

Print the test part

Print the test piece and a guide pin (pecas/comum/stl/comum-pino-guia.stl). The drawing below shows each hole from above. Beside the micro servo window are three insert holes (3,9, 4,0 and 4,1 mm), and at the tip is the larger hole for the yellow motor shaft (the shaft must turn freely). The other row has three guide-pin sockets (clearances of 0,20, 0,25 and 0,30 mm) and four screw holes: M3 that passes freely, M3 that threads into the plastic (M3* in the drawing), M2,5 and M2. The design uses the 4,0 mm hole and the 0,25 mm socket, the middle ones, marked with a dimple (● in the drawing). The M3 insert (the brass sleeve that goes in hot, with a soldering iron, and provides threads) must fit snugly; the pin must fit firmly without hammering. If both fit, continue. If a neighboring size fits better, adjust the clearance in cad/parametros.py, in the FOLGA block (componente for the servo, encaixe for the pin, inserto_d for the insert), and regenerate from step 3: that’s the only time you need Python.

# open this file in the slicer
pecas/comum/stl/comum-teste-encaixe.stl

view the test part on GitHub (download button on the page)

3

Generate and check (only if you changed a measurement)

The first command recreates all the parts; the second runs the 9 tests and exits with an error if any fail.

python3 -m venv .venv
.venv/bin/pip install -r requirements.txt
# takes a few minutes
.venv/bin/python cad/gerar.py todos
.venv/bin/python cad/verificar.py todos

These commands are for Linux and macOS. On Windows, use python instead of python3, dir instead of ls, .venv\Scripts\pip instead of .venv/bin/pip, and .venv\Scripts\python instead of .venv/bin/python.

4

Print the build plates

Each build plate below is one print: the parts are already laid flat, grouped by color and material, in a 3MF file with the geometry (the file doesn’t include slicer settings and hasn’t been opened in a slicer yet; if yours won’t open it, use the individual STL files in pecas/<modelo>/stl and the table). The filename is <modelo>-mesa-NN-material-cor.3mf. Untested starting point: 0,2 mm layer height, 0,4 mm nozzle, 3 walls and 15 % infill. The rounded shells need tree supports; the skeleton prints without supports, except for berco-tilt. The test piece and guide pins are repeated on the gray build plate: if you’ve already printed them, remove them from the plate. Colors are just for appearance: the minimum is one roll of PETG for the skeleton and one of PLA for the shell. In the tables, mesa-cabeca is the name of a part (the one that supports the head from the inside).

# one 3MF file per build plate, for each model
pecas/dino/mesas/     pecas/capivara/mesas/     pecas/cubo/mesas/

5

Assemble one of the animals

Assembly order for the dinosaur and capybara:

  1. Heat-set the M3 threaded inserts into the parts.
  2. Screw the two motors onto the chassis walls (the large dark part, which is the skeleton), from the outside, with the motor facing up.
  3. Attach the support ball with its cover underneath.
  4. Mount the head-turning servo on top of the chassis, then the fork, the second servo, and the cradle (the two neck parts).
  5. Put the battery and board trays in from the back.
  6. Close the torso from the front, then fit the wheels from the sides.
  7. Belly panel, back, legs or thighs, tail.
  8. Assemble the head on the workbench (the mesa-cabeca part goes under the skull; then add the mouth servo, jaw, and eyes) and lower the whole head onto the neck: two screws from above, hidden under the crest.
  9. Spikes, claws, ears, and bandana are press-fit or glued in place.
6

Or assemble the square robot

Assembly order:

  1. Servo on the base floor and Arduino beside it; then the large gear on top of the servo.
  2. Insert the battery through the rear door.
  3. Neck tube under the cover, then the cover on the base.
  4. Fork on top of the tube, with three long screws.
  5. Raspberry Pi, screen, and the second servo on the head frame.
  6. Head shell from the rear, screen frame from the front.
  7. Head between the fork arms and the rear door.
